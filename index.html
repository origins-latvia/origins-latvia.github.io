Preface

This all began as an attempt to translate Dad’s diaries for any of his descendants who at some stage might be interested in them. It developed into the story of how Helēna Mackevica and Jānis Avens came to start a new life in Australia. As such their story forms part of the larger one of those displaced persons who migrated to Australia in the aftermath of WWII.

Whilst Dad’s travels are comparatively well documented, Mum’s are not, even to the extent that the file related to the voyage of the Protea, which brought her here, is no longer extant in the National Archives of Australia (NAA). For this reason her part in the narrative is unfortunately minor.

In the diary transcriptions which follow I try to represent what Dad actually wrote, whether or not I believe the spelling or details to be correct - spelling mistakes in the English translation or commentaries / annotations are mine alone.

Prologue

Jānis Avens, son of Jānis (b.1875) and Emīlija Gaigale was born in Kosa, Latvija in 1914. Kosa is barely a speck on the map about 100km from Rīga and an hour by foot from the parish centre Skujene. It is 33km from the Provincial capital Cēsis.

Their house was called Skuķi and it was from that that Dad's Grandmother Emelīne Šarlote (n. Bernharde) got her nickname: Skuķu Māte (the old lady from Skuķi).

Because Context is Everthing ....

Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact

It started on August 23, 1939 with the Treaty of Non-Aggression between Germany and the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics whereby the 20th century’s greatest monsters, Hitler and Stalin divided up Europe between them and effectively began WWII.

Soviet occupation of Latvia in 1940-41: Baigais Gads / The Terrible Year

“June 17, 1940 - Soviet troops invade Latvia and occupy bridges, post/telephone, telegraph, and broadcasting offices .... The Soviet authorities, having gained control over Latvia, immediately imposed a regime of terror. Hundreds .... were arrested, including many leaders of the Republic of Latvia. Tribunals were set up to punish “traitors to the people.”.... The June deportation took place on June 13 and June 14, 1941, estimated at 15,600 men, women, and children, and including 20% of Latvia’s last legal government. Approximately 35,000 total (1.8% of Latvia’s population) were deported during the first Soviet occupation.”[1]

German occupation of Latvia in 1941-45

On 22 June, 1941 Hitler betrayed his co-conspirator Stalin and launched Operation Barbarossa, the invasion of Russia by Germany. Given the Russian reign of terror, the Germans were at first greeted as liberators by most Latvians. But they were about to be deceived. As the tide turned against Germany, illegal conscription of citizens of occupied countries was implemented. In Latvia this eventually lead to the formation of the medaciously named Latvian SS-Volunteer Legion.

Hague Convention (October 18, 1907): “It is forbidden to compel the inhabitants of occupied territory to swear allegiance to the hostile Power.”[2]

“The law of nations and international precedent .... is interpreted as an injunction against the implementation of mandatory military conscription policies over the local populace....”

“.... compulsory military conscription under such circumstances [also] violates Articles 40, 45, and 51 of the “Geneva Convention ....” (1949).[3]

“The punishment for draft evasion: “At a time when each true Latvian man who can carry a weapon is standing guard over his fatherland, three strong young men have chosen a different route... The Special War Tribunal of the General Inspectorate of the Latvian SS Volunteer Legion heard evidence against Edgars Vilciņš, Osvalds Streļķis and Jānis Vilciņš... The Special War Tribunal sentenced 26 year old Edgars Vilciņš and 23 year old Osvalds Streļķis to 15 years of hard labour...” (Tēvija, 20 September 1944)[4]
“In accordance with Gen. Jenkel’s order Lieut. Col. Voldemārs Ginters, at the end of Oct. 1943 commenced formation of Latvian Police Battalion 319-F (319-F Schutzmannschaft Battalion) based on national guards nominated by district chairmen [such as Kārlis Ezergailis in Kosa] .... Troops comprising the Battalion on the whole had already served in the Latvian Army, however they were of various ages and conscripted without medical examination.”[5]

“In 1946, the Nuremberg Tribunal declared the Waffen-SS to be a criminal organization, making an exception of people who were forcibly conscripted. Throughout the post-war years, Allies would apply this exception to the soldiers of the Latvian Legion and the Estonian Legion. The US Displaced Persons Commission in September 1950 declared that:

"The Baltic Waffen SS Units (Baltic Legions) are to be considered as separate and distinct in purpose, ideology, activities, and qualifications for membership from the German SS, and therefore the Commission holds them not to be a movement hostile to the Government of the United States."[6]

When it was all over with millions dead for no reason as usual, Hitler died a cowards death by suicide on 30 April, 1945. His co-conspirator Stalin proclaimed himself the saviour of Europe and continued his murderous ways until eventually meeting his maker in Hell on 5 March, 1953. The second Russian occupation of Latvia, bringing with it further deportations, lasted until the disintegration of the USSR in 1991 and the departure of the last Russian troups from Latvian soil on August 31, 1994.

It is said that Latvians fought on both side of War II: they all lost.

Jānis Avens’ life was to take an unimaginable change of course on 22 October, 1943.
